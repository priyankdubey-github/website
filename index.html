About Me

I am a third year Ph.D. student in Systems Engineering at Texas A&M University, working in the SEAK Lab with Dr. Daniel Selva. My research focuses on translating foundational quantum advances into deployable tools for real-world applications in the broad area of search, optimization, and planning.

Quantum computing offers a fundamentally new way to approach some of the hardest computational problems. Turning that promise into working algorithms is a different challenge. Today's NISQ devices are limited by qubit count, device topology, and gate errors, which makes realistic-scale algorithms hard to validate, let alone deploy. One option is to keep designing algorithms and wait a decade to verify them. The other is to be smart about it by treating these hardware limits as design constraints and build algorithms that can be tested now. My research follows this second path through two tightly coupled questions:

  • Translation: How do we systematically map large, complex real-world problems onto Hamiltonians or parameterized circuits so the formulation itself is built for quantum execution?
  • Fit: How do we design these formulations and algorithms so they stay within a device's qubit budget, connectivity, and noise tolerance, while still solving the original problem?

The result is hardware-aware algorithm design, built for real devices and tested on real problems.

Research

Quantum methods that leave the simulator

A large body of theory exists across quantum algorithms, sensing, and information. Much of its value depends on whether it can be engineered for problems at real scale. That gap between theory and deployability is where I work.

I use space systems as the near-term application domain. Limited onboard resources, harsh operating environments, and the need for precise sensing and autonomous operation in deep space make them a demanding testbed for finding where near-term quantum methods deliver practical value. My long-term vision, which I call Quantronautics, is to design space systems from quantum principles rather than adding quantum components to classical designs.

Quantum Optimization for Real-World Systems

I build quantum and hybrid quantum-classical solvers (Simulated Quantum Annealing, QAOA, VQE) by translating real combinatorial and continuous problems into quantum-compatible formulations.

At NASA JPL I developed a hybrid Simulated Quantum Annealing solver in Julia, based on a Suzuki-Trotter path-integral formulation, for Deep Space Network scheduling. The DSN coordinates tracking and communication for more than 40 deep-space missions and is often oversubscribed. The solver produced schedules with more satisfied requests, faster than MILP, machine-learning, and prior approaches. A journal article is in preparation.

Also: QAOA/VQE for satellite routing in debris collection and on-orbit servicing.

Renormalization Group Methods for NISQ Tractability

Real problems often exceed the qubit count and connectivity of today's hardware. I use Renormalization Group theory from statistical physics to find which degrees of freedom matter for a target observable and integrate out the rest. The result is a compact, NISQ-compatible model that keeps classical solution quality.

The coarse-graining also reveals structure on its own, such as coordination timescales and network vulnerabilities, independent of the quantum solver used afterwards.

Applied to cislunar space situational awareness, constellation design, and coverage optimization.

Quantum Walk Algorithms for Graph Inference

Quantum walks spread amplitude along a graph's own topology. Paths aligned with the structure interfere constructively and irrelevant paths cancel, so arrival probabilities encode both local and global structure.

This led to QSearchNet, which combines topology-aware quantum walks with Grover's amplitude amplification for link prediction and network-evolution forecasting. I later extended it with adaptive quantum teleportation for sparse networks, capturing long-range correlations while preserving local structure.

Multi-Node Quantum-Classical Sensing Networks

In QuaSense I study how Rydberg-state quantum sensors can work alongside existing classical space infrastructure, through three architectures: locally enhanced arrays, distributed entangled sensing, and capability-based data fusion.

The goal is an actionable framework for designing, tasking, and tuning a mixed network of quantum and classical nodes toward a single shared mission objective, for Earth observation and space situational awareness.
